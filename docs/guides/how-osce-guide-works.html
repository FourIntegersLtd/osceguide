Chapter 1

What OSCE Guide is

A place to practise and run the spoken, face-to-face exams of medicine, with an AI patient on the other side of the conversation.

Doctors are tested on more than what they know. In an OSCE (objective structured clinical examination) a candidate walks into a room, reads a short brief, and has a few minutes to talk to a patient: take a history, explain a diagnosis, agree a plan. An examiner watches and marks them against a fixed set of criteria. Then a bell rings and the candidate moves to the next room. A full exam is a circuit of these rooms, called stations.

OSCE Guide recreates that experience. The patient is played by an AI that speaks and listens, so a candidate can practise at any hour. Each conversation is recorded word for word and marked against the same kind of criteria a human examiner uses. Some exams covered today are the UK licensing exam for overseas doctors (PLAB Part 2) and the general practice licensing exam (the SCA).

Three groups of people use the product, and they use the same engine underneath.

Two more groups stand beside these. Partners are organisations that send people to us, such as a publisher or a course provider; they can see how many people they sent and what those people bought. Our own team sets organisations up, answers support questions and keeps the service healthy (chapter 6).

The rule behind most of the product: records are kept

A school may have to justify a pass or a fail to a student, an appeal panel or a regulator, months or years later. So the product switches things off rather than deleting them. A student who leaves has their access revoked. A member of staff who moves on loses their role. A school whose contract ends is suspended. A published station is archived, not deleted, and a corrected one becomes a new version. The one real deletion is personal: when a person closes their own account, they have 30 days to change their mind, and after that their personal details, and what they said in their recordings, are wiped.

One consultation, start to finish

Everything else in this guide is built on one thing: a candidate talking to a patient at one station, and what happens to that conversation afterwards.

WhoEvery user: a doctor alone, a student in a school, a candidate for a job
How longA few minutes of reading, then usually ten of talking
StatusWorks today

Inside a station

A station is one medical case, written down once and used for years. Different people are allowed to read different parts of it. The candidate sees only their brief. The AI patient is given its story, including details it should reveal only if asked the right question. The examiner's notes and the material for the after-session tutor are kept away from the candidate.

Stations are written on a station template, which sets the headings every station of that kind has. Stations change in versions. A draft can be edited freely. A published version is never changed in place; a correction becomes a new version, so every recording points at exactly the wording the candidate saw.

What happens in a session

  1. Open. The product checks the person may use this station. For someone practising alone, that means a live plan or a trial with room left. For a school's exam, it means being on that circuit. Exam-day sessions never check a plan.
  2. The room opens. The product books a seat on the voice service and dispatches the AI patient with only the parts of the station it may read.
  3. The consultation. Speech is turned into text as the candidate speaks, the patient's reply is generated, and the reply is spoken back. Everything said is written down.
  4. It ends. The transcript is stored with the exact station wording frozen as it was at that moment. A session left running is closed automatically after three hours.
  5. Marking. The automatic marker reads the transcript and rates each area of the marking scheme. The candidate sees ratings and written feedback, and can open the whole transcript.

The life of a recording

How marking works

A marking scheme is the instrument a consultation is judged against. It has a scale (for example red, amber and green), the areas being judged (such as gathering information, management, and communication), and the exact wording that earns each rating in each area. Schemes are published in versions, like stations. An exam names one published version of a scheme, and that is what every consultation for that exam is marked against.

The automatic marker rates each area separately. The overall rating for the consultation is the worst of the area ratings: a green for communication does not rescue a red for safety. In a school, people can add their own ratings to each area, and how those combine is explained in chapter 4.

What can go wrong, and what you would see

  • The voice service cannot be reached. The session does not open. You read: "The voice service is not available right now."
  • Rejoining a session that has already finished, for example from an old tab. You read: "That attempt has already finished."
  • A step is asked for out of order, such as finishing a session that never started. You read: "The attempt is not in a state that allows this."
  • Opening the feedback while the consultation is still to come. The review tab says "not yet" rather than failing: "This attempt has not finished, so there is nothing to review yet."
  • The station has not been prepared for the session yet, usually for a few seconds after opening it. You read: "This attempt has no rendered definition yet."
  • Following a link to a session that does not exist, or belongs to another organisation. You read: "No such attempt."
  • Opening somebody else's session without being an administrator allowed to see it. You read: "That attempt belongs to someone else."
  • Starting a station whose marking scheme is not published. Refused at the start, so nobody sits a station that could never be marked. You read: "This station cannot be marked yet: its rubric is not published."
  • Something tries to mark against a draft scheme. You read: "That rubric version is not published."
  • The automatic marker is switched off on this server. Nothing can be marked until it is back. You read: "Marking is not available on this server."
  • Too many tabs are open on the same account, each holding a live connection. You read: "Too many live connections are open for this account. Close another tab and try again."

Stage 1

Finding us

Most people arrive through a link from somebody else: a partner such as a publisher or course, or a friend who already uses the product.

  1. A partner's link. When someone arrives through a partner's link, the product notes which partner sent them. If they sign up afterwards, the partner is credited with that person, once. There is no time limit: any visit through the link before signing up counts. A link naming a partner we do not know credits nobody.
  2. A friend's referral code. Every user has a referral code. A friend who types it when paying gets the plan's referral price, which is 10% off the full price, and the person who shared the code is credited and told by email.
  3. The public website. Anyone can read about the product and send us a message through the contact form, signed in or not.

What can go wrong, and what you would see

  • A referral code that does not exist, or is the person's own. The payment screen says: "That referral code is not valid. Check it, or clear it to pay the full price."
  • A good code on a plan with no referral price. Refused rather than charging full price to someone expecting a discount: "That plan has no referral discount. Choose another plan, or clear the code."
  • A contact message missing something it needs, such as an email address to reply to. You read: "That message cannot be sent as written."
Stage 2

Signing up and the free trial

A new account needs an email address, a password and a choice of exam. The exam decides which stations, study material and practice partners the person sees.

  1. Choose a password. At least 12 characters, using at least two kinds of character out of lower case, upper case, numbers and symbols.
  2. Choose an exam. This is set once. Changing it later is a support request, because it changes what the person has paid for.
  3. Start practising. The trial begins at once. When it runs out, the library still shows, but starting a new station asks the person to subscribe or ask their organisation for access.

What can go wrong, and what you would see

  • Signing up with an address that already has an account. The form marks the email field: "That email address is already registered."
  • A password that is too short or too simple. The form marks the password field: "Choose a stronger password."
  • Trying to change exam from the account screen. You read: "Your exam is already set. Contact support to switch exams."
  • The trial is used up, or a plan has ended. One refusal, whatever the reason; the screen picks the wording. Trial over: "Your free practice stations are used up. Ask your organisation for access, or subscribe, to keep practising." Plan ended: "Your access to this exam has ended. Ask your organisation to renew it, or subscribe, to keep practising."
Stage 3

The first consultation

The person picks a station from the library for their exam and goes through the five stages of chapter 2.

  1. Browse. The library lists the published stations for the person's exam, searchable and in pages.
  2. Start. The product checks the trial or plan, opens a room and sends the patient in.
  3. Resume. If the person leaves mid-consultation and comes back, a session still running is offered back to them to resume, and an unstarted one is reused rather than duplicated.

What can go wrong, and what you would see

  • A station that has been withdrawn, or was never finished. You read: "This station is not available to practise right now. It may have been withdrawn or not finished yet."
  • A link to a station that does not exist in the library or the person's organisation. You read: "No such station."
  • Every try at a station in a practice list is used. You read: "You have used every attempt at this station."
Stage 4

Reading the feedback, then the debrief

The marking arrives on screen a short while after the consultation ends. The person can then talk it through with an AI tutor.

  1. Feedback. A rating for each area of the marking scheme, the overall rating, written comments, and the full transcript.
  2. Debrief. A spoken conversation with an AI tutor about a finished consultation. The tutor uses the station's teaching notes, asks how it felt, and works through what to do differently. It may play the patient once so the person can try a moment again.
  3. Memory. After each debrief, a short summary is kept. The tutor uses these summaries to remember what the person has struggled with before, and the person can read them back in their debrief history.

What can go wrong, and what you would see

  • Asking for a debrief on a station never attempted. You read: "A debrief needs a recorded consultation for this station. Attempt the station first."
  • Opening a debrief summary that is not yours, or no longer exists. Another person's gets the same answer: "No such debrief."
  • The tutor looks up a remembered note that is not there. The tutor carries on without it: "No such note."
Stage 5

Paying

Access is bought per exam, as a plan of a fixed length. Prices and lengths are set by us as data, not built into the product, so they can change without a release.

  1. Same plan again. Buying a plan you already hold, running or bought to start, is refused. A different plan is allowed, and it starts where the current access ends, so time is never lost.
  2. Refunds. If a payment is refunded, the access it bought is withdrawn, and any referral credit it earned is taken back.
  3. Disputes. If a card holder disputes a payment with their bank, its access is withdrawn while the dispute runs. If we win, what the dispute took is given back.
  4. Receipts. Each payment has a receipt the person can open again from their account.

What can go wrong, and what you would see

  • Buying the same plan while it is still running. You read: "You already have this plan. Choose a different plan to extend your access, or buy it again when it ends."
  • A plan that is no longer on sale, or not for your exam. You read: "That plan is not available for your exam. Choose another plan."
  • The card provider cannot start a payment. Usually brief. You read: "We could not start the checkout. Try again in a moment."
  • A receipt link that is not yours, or does not exist. Both get the same answer, so receipt numbers cannot be guessed: "No such receipt."

Stage 7

Practising with a real person

Two doctors preparing for the same exam meet in an online room. One plays the doctor, the other the patient, using a published station.

Who plays the doctor first

The product decides, in this order. Someone who has asked never to play the doctor plays the patient. If the two last played opposite roles, they swap. Someone who has never practised this way plays the doctor before someone who has. Otherwise, whoever has done fewer sessions plays the doctor. A person can never be paired with themselves.

  1. Recording. A session can be recorded, transcribed and scored like a consultation with the AI patient. Its states are: not recording, recording, stopped, being transcribed, scored, failed, or erased.
  2. Feedback. Once a session that began has finished, each person can leave feedback for the other.
  3. Reports. A person can report abuse or a partner who did not turn up. Both are emailed to our team.

What can go wrong, and what you would see

  • Joining the queue twice. You keep your place: "You're already in the practice queue."
  • Leaving a queue you are no longer in. You read: "You are not in the queue."
  • Your exam has no published station yet. You read: "There are no stations to practise for that exam yet."
  • The other person declined, or the 120 seconds ran out. You read: "Your partner is no longer available for this session."
  • Trying to join before both sides accepted. You read: "Both sides must accept this match before joining it."
  • Acting on a session that has finished. You read: "That session has already finished."
  • A session you were not part of. You read: "No such practice session."
  • Leaving feedback before the session finished, or on one that never began. You read: "Feedback opens once a session you took part in has finished."
  • Someone else claimed the slot a moment before you. You read: "This slot has already been claimed by someone else."
  • The slot was withdrawn, expired or has started. You read: "That slot is no longer available."
  • Claiming your own slot. You read: "You cannot claim your own slot."
  • Withdrawing a slot someone else offered. You read: "Only the person who offered a slot can withdraw it."
  • Joining more than 20 minutes before the start. The session is fine; come back closer to the time: "That session has not opened yet."
  • Joining a cancelled session, or more than an hour after the start. You read: "That session can no longer be joined."
  • A booked session that does not exist. You read: "No such session."

Stage 9

Your account: signing in, security and leaving

The same account is used on every side of the product. These rules apply to everyone.

  1. Signing in. Email and password. After 10 tries in a minute on one account, or 300 from one address, the product asks the person to wait.
  2. Forgotten password. A reset link by email, valid for 60 minutes and usable once. At most 5 requests in 5 minutes.
  3. Two-step sign-in. An authenticator app gives a six-digit code at each sign-in. Ten one-use recovery codes are given for a lost phone, and can be replaced. An organisation can require it for its staff. Students and people practising alone are never forced to use it. People who sign in through their organisation's own system skip it, because that system checks the second step.
  4. Changing email. A confirmation link goes to the new address, and a notice goes to the old one.
  5. Signing in through an organisation. Some schools connect their own sign-in system. Their members then sign in there instead of with a password.
  6. Closing an account. Sign-in stops at once and every device is signed out. Signing in again with the password within 30 days reopens the account, and an email confirms it. After 30 days the account is wiped: name, email, roster entries, the text of results, and what was said in recordings. An email confirms that too.

What can go wrong, and what you would see

  • A wrong password, or an address we do not know. Both get the same answer, so nobody can test which addresses have accounts: "Email or password is incorrect."
  • Too many tries in a short time. You read: "Too many attempts. Wait a moment and try again."
  • A member of an organisation that uses its own sign-in, trying a password. You read: "This account signs in through your organisation. Use your organisation's sign-in instead."
  • An account that has been closed or switched off. You read: "This account is not active."
  • Still signed in on a device after the account was wiped. You read: "Sign in again."
  • A sign-in that has expired or was ended elsewhere. You read: "Sign in again."
  • Opening a page that needs you to be signed in. You are taken to sign in: "Authentication required."
  • Signing in with a temporary password a colleague passed on. You are sent straight to choose your own: "Set a new password before continuing."
  • A reset link that is old or already used. You read: "That link is no longer valid. Ask for a new one and use the most recent email." For an invitation link: "That invitation has already been used or has expired."
  • Changing a password on an account that never had one, such as one that only signs in through its organisation. You read: "This account has no password yet. Ask for a reset link to set one."
  • A wrong or reused six-digit code. You read: "That code is not valid."
  • Taking too long between password and code. You read: "That took too long, so sign in again."
  • Setting up two-step sign-in twice. You read: "An authenticator is already set up."
  • Turning off two-step sign-in that was never on. You read: "This account has no authenticator set up."
  • Turning off two-step sign-in your organisation requires. You read: "Two-factor sign-in is required for this account, so it cannot be turned off."
  • An email-change link that is old, used, or for an address someone has taken since. You read: "That link has expired or has already been used."
  • A profile photo that is not a small image. You read: "That file cannot be used as a profile photo."

Stage 1

Setting up the school

A school cannot sign itself up. Our team creates it after a contract is agreed, then hands it over.

The eight setup steps, in order

1. A marking scheme. 2. An exam. 3. A programme. 4. A station template. 5. Stations. 6. The team. 7. Trainees. 8. The first sitting. Each step needs the ones before it: a sitting needs a programme, a programme needs an exam, and an exam needs a marking scheme.

  1. Inviting the team. An administrator invites supervisors and other administrators by email. If an invitation runs out, it can be resent. Someone who has already chosen a password gets a reset link instead.

  • Resending an invitation to someone who has already joined, or who signs in through the school's system. You read: "There is nothing to resend for that member."
  • Giving a temporary password to someone who has not yet accepted their invitation. You read: "This person has not set a password yet. Resend their invitation instead."

  • Acting in a school whose access has been suspended. Members can sign in, but cannot act: "This organisation's access has been suspended."
  • A web address for a school that does not exist. You read: "No such organisation."
  • Claiming a web address or email domain another organisation already uses. You read: "Another organisation already claims that host or email domain."
  • An address or domain that is not one. You read: "That is not a usable hostname or email domain."
  • Turning off passwords while some members only have a password. The screen lists how many would be affected: "Some members sign in with a password and would be locked out."
  • Settings that would leave nobody a way in, such as turning off passwords while the school's own sign-in is off. You read: "That combination of settings would leave nobody a way in."
  • Naming a sign-in provider we do not know. You read: "That is not an identity provider this platform knows."
  • A sign-in connection missing its address or certificate. You read: "That SAML connection is not usable."
  • A sign-in connection that has been switched off or removed. You read: "No such SAML connection."
  • Signing in through a school that has no sign-in system connected, or has switched it off. You read: "That organisation does not sign in through an identity provider."
  • The school's sign-in system did not vouch for you, usually an interrupted login. You read: "That sign-in could not be verified. Try again."
  • A school whose sign-in provider we know of but have not built yet. You read: "That organisation's sign-in provider is not enabled here."
  • The service that links to school sign-in systems cannot be reached. Nobody is let in by mistake: "Sign-in through an identity provider is not available here."
Stage 2

Exams, marking schemes and stations

Before anyone can be examined, the school needs an exam to run, a scheme to mark it against, and stations to put in it. It can write its own, or bring in ours.

  1. Writing a station. Write by hand, start from an uploaded document, or ask the AI to draft one from a brief. A station can have pictures, such as an X-ray or a rash, in the places its template allows.
  2. Fact check. Before publishing, the AI can check the station's medical facts and mark anything doubtful for a person to look at.
  3. Publishing. A draft with nothing in it cannot go live. Publishing the next version leaves earlier recordings pointing at the version they used.
  4. Retiring. A published version is archived rather than deleted, and only a draft can be deleted. An exam that has anything recorded against it is retired, never deleted.
  5. Reports. Anyone using a station can report a problem with it. The report goes to its authors.

What can go wrong, and what you would see

  • Two exams with the same short code. You read: "An exam with that code already exists."

Stage 3

Candidates arrive: the roster

The roster is the school's list of its trainees. A circuit can only examine people on it.

  1. Adding people. One at a time, or from a spreadsheet. A spreadsheet can hold up to 500 people per upload; the screen sends larger lists in batches. Someone already on the list is skipped, not added twice. Each new trainee gets an invitation email.
  2. Cohorts and groups. A cohort is a year group, and a trainee is in at most one. Groups are any other way of sorting people, such as by hospital site, and a trainee can be in several.
  3. Automatic lists. A school with its own sign-in system can keep the roster in step with it automatically. Changes flow from the school's own records, so nobody re-types them.
  4. Practice access. A school can also give trainees access to practise alone (chapter 3) for an exam, until a date it chooses, instead of each trainee paying.
  5. Leavers. Removing someone's access is how a person leaves. Their records stay. A trainee who has a decision recorded cannot be deleted, only have their access removed.

What can go wrong, and what you would see

  • Adding someone already on the list. You read: "That person is already on your trainee list."
  • More than 500 people in one upload. The screen normally splits the list itself: "An import takes at most 500 trainees at a time."
  • A trainee who has been removed. You read: "That trainee is no longer on your list."
  • Putting someone whose access was removed into a sitting. You read: "That person's access has been removed. Restore them before adding them to a sitting."
  • Deleting a trainee who has a decision recorded. You read: "This trainee has a decision recorded and cannot be deleted. Revoke access instead."
  • Examining someone who is not on the school's list. You read: "That person is not on your trainee list. Add them first."
  • Two year groups with the same name. You read: "A cohort with that name already exists."
  • Deleting a year group that still has trainees. You read: "That cohort still has trainees in it."
  • A year group that has been deleted. You read: "That cohort no longer exists."
  • Two groups with the same name. You read: "A group with that name already exists."
  • Deleting a group that still has members. You read: "That group still has members."
  • A group that has been deleted. You read: "That group no longer exists."
  • Giving practice access to something that is not an email address. A spreadsheet reports it per row: "That is not a valid email address."
  • Giving practice access to someone who already has it from this school for this exam. You read: "That person already has practice access to this exam."
  • Changing practice access that has ended or was never given. You read: "No such entitlement in this organisation."

Stage 5

Exam day

The supervisor presses one button to start the circuit. From then on the product runs the clock, opens each room, sends each patient in and moves everyone on.

What a supervisor can do while it runs

Pause and resume the clock, give extra time, restart the clock on a station, send the patient in again, let a candidate redo a station, jump to a station, move everyone on now, or let the circuit move on by itself. Every one of these is saved with the name of the person who did it and the reason they gave.

  1. The minute check. Every minute the product checks every live circuit. A patient who has gone quiet for 60 seconds is sent in again.
  2. Forgotten circuits. A circuit left untouched for 12 hours is closed by itself, unless it is paused on purpose.

What can go wrong, and what you would see

  • Acting on a circuit that has not been started. You read: "The circuit has not been started yet."
  • Joining a circuit that is not running, because it has not begun or is over. You read: "The circuit is not running, so nothing can be sat on it now."
  • Moving on while the clock is paused. You read: "The circuit is paused. Resume it first."
  • Resuming a clock that is running. You read: "The circuit is not paused."
  • Acting on a circuit that has finished. You read: "The circuit has already ended."
  • Stepping into a station during a break, when none is running. You read: "No station is being sat on this circuit right now."
  • Jumping to a station this circuit does not have. You read: "That station is not on this circuit."
  • A candidate's place on the circuit that no longer exists. You read: "That place on the circuit no longer exists. Reload the page."
  • Doing something your role does not allow, such as a candidate opening a supervisor's screen. You read: "You do not have access to this resource."
Stage 6

Marking

Every recording is marked by the AI first, exactly as in chapter 2. In a school, people then review it, and their ratings are what count.

  1. Who reviews. The supervisor who led the circuit, or an administrator. Each person writes one review per candidate per station.
  2. Drafts. A review stays a draft until every area is rated. Its author can withdraw a draft; a completed review is edited instead, never withdrawn.
  3. Senior review. An administrator can add a senior review on top, with a reason of up to 4,000 characters. If someone else saved it first, the screen asks you to reload rather than overwrite them.
  4. Finalising. Once every review on a circuit is done, the circuit's review is finalised. After a decision is signed off, its reviews are fixed.

What can go wrong, and what you would see

  • Reviewing a circuit you did not lead, without being an administrator. You read: "Only the supervisor named on this circuit, or an administrator, can review it."
  • Completing a review with an area unrated. You read: "Rate every required area before completing the review."
  • A rating the scheme does not use. You read: "One of the ratings is not one this station accepts. Pick again from the list."
  • Saving a review as something other than a draft or completed. You read: "A review can only be saved as a draft or completed."
  • Someone else changed the review while you had it open. You read: "Someone else changed this review while you had it open. Reload the page and make your changes again, so theirs are not lost."
  • Withdrawing a completed review. You read: "Only a draft review can be withdrawn. A completed review is edited instead."
  • Changing a review after the result was signed off. You read: "This candidate's result has been signed off or released, so its reviews can no longer be changed."
  • A station nobody took, or with no areas to rate. You read: "Nobody took this station, so there is nothing to review."
  • Finalising a circuit's review twice. You read: "This circuit's review has already been finalised."
  • A review that has been withdrawn. You read: "That review no longer exists. Reload the page."

Stage 8

Term after term

After the exam, the school reads its results, moves people on, and renews its contract.

  1. Reports. A summary per exam sitting shows how the group did, station by station and area by area. Training sessions get feedback, not a group summary.
  2. Leavers and new years. New trainees join the roster; leavers have their access removed. Their past records stay.
  3. Contract end. When the contract's end date passes, an hourly check suspends the school. Its members can no longer act in it. Our team renews it with a new end date, which must be in the future, and everything is as it was.

What can go wrong, and what you would see

  • Asking for a group summary of a training session. You read: "A cohort summary is only produced for an exam sitting."
  • Renewing with an end date already passed. It would lapse again within the hour: "A renewal must end in the future."
  • A contract that ends before it starts. You read: "The end date is before the start date."

Partners: sending people to us

Chapter 9

What is still rough

The places we know are not finished. Each is tracked, and this list is taken from our own record of gaps.

  • Rough One at a time. Removing several team members, or resending or removing several trainees, has to be done one person at a time on screen, though the product could do it in one step.
  • Open Undecided results. The results screen shows how many decisions are undecided. Whether it should also show a score column is waiting for a walk-through on the test copy of the product.
  • Open An examiner role. Whether to add a separate role for examiners who only review, beside supervisor and administrator, is a product decision still to make.
  • Open Remove, delete or wipe. The roster offers three ways to deal with a leaver. Explanations are on screen; whether to fold the practice-access tab into each person's row is still to decide.
  • Open Starting to practise. Whether a session is opened before the person has chosen to begin is waiting for a check in a real browser.

Written for anyone who wants to understand how OSCE Guide works. Every "what can go wrong" row is checked against the product's own list of refusals, so a refusal added to the product without a row here fails our checks.
