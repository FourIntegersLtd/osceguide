# Teaching presence to AI patients without headsets

No product or study in 2026 shows that a trainee can learn physical presence (sitting close, touch, body language) from a virtual AI patient, and the palliative team's concern is therefore well founded. What the evidence does support is narrower: verbal AI-patient practice improves serious-illness communication behaviours, learners say the missing pauses, vocal emotion and visual cues limit realism, and the one physical behaviour with randomised evidence (sitting) is cheap to rehearse in a real room. The most defensible plan is therefore hybrid. Keep the browser and LiveKit stack for words, timing and silence. Add low-inference browser features (stage directions, "action" buttons, audio-derived silence metrics, an optional on-device webcam prompt). Run a pilot in which the AI voice comes from a speaker at a real bedside or chair so the trainee physically sits. Treat talking-head avatars as a moderate-cost experiment, and VR or haptics as a later bet with thin evidence and high cost. Much of the underlying literature here is abstract-only, preprint or vendor-authored, and the report flags this wherever it applies.

## The evidence for physical presence is real but narrow, and mostly not about training

The best-quantified non-verbal behaviour is sitting. In a randomised video study of 69 inpatient oncology patients, **51% preferred the sitting physician, 23% the standing one**, and patients rated the sitting doctor as more compassionate and as having spent longer, although actual time was similar ([KSSG abstract](https://forschung.kssg.ch/en/publications/830-impact-of-physician-sitting-versus-standing-during-inpatient-oncology-consultations-patients-preference-and-perception-of-compassion-and-duration-a-randomized-controlled-trial)). A separate randomised trial of posture when breaking bad news also found sitting preferred ([MD Anderson record](https://mdanderson.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/a-randomized-controlled-trial-of-physician-postures-when-breaking)). Both are conference abstracts using video vignettes, so they show patient perception, not better end-of-life outcomes. Evidence for touch is weaker still, limited to a small pilot in which patients felt empathic touch helped coping ([Univ. of the Free State](https://scholar.ufs.ac.za/items/cc89cc92-4b07-489a-b4e5-3544aa1aefc9)), and silence is described as multifunctional but poorly understood ([Lancaster](https://www.research.lancs.ac.uk/portal/en/publications/silence-as-an-element-of-care(21b7a448-6143-46fc-b896-4f6b08e4e90a).html)). Clinicians themselves already name touch, proximity and looking as their main non-verbal strategies, though in a 2008-09 Brazilian survey only 15.2% could list five ([Rev Esc Enferm USP](https://revistas.usp.br/reeusp/en/article/view/40990)).

The frameworks do not fill the gap. The VitalTalk NURSE page contains no guidance on sitting, touch or silence ([VitalTalk](https://www.vitaltalk.org/guides/responding-to-emotion/)), and the snippets of GMC and NICE NG142 end-of-life guidance are value-based rather than behavioural ([GMC](https://www.gmc-uk.org/professional-standards/the-professional-standards/treatment-and-care-towards-the-end-of-life/about-this-guidance), [NICE](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/NG142)). The palliative team will therefore have to define its own non-verbal rubric, and the product should let it do so. SAGE & THYME, taught to over 60,000 UK staff, reports gains in knowledge, confidence and behaviour at two months, but I found nothing on structured non-verbal scoring in UK programmes ([Manchester FT](https://mft.nhs.uk/medical-education/sage-thyme-training/)).

## AI patients teach words and turn-taking well, and learners name exactly what is missing

The closest evidence to the OSCE Guide product is positive but verbal. In the SOPHIE randomised trial (LLM, avatar, automated feedback), learners improved more than a reading control on Empower (17% vs 6%), Be Explicit (13% vs 5%) and Empathize (14% vs 7%), but this is a preprint with no visible sample size or non-verbal assessment ([arXiv 2505.02694](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.02694)). The PAL palliative chatbot study (n=17) found 12 learners preferred voice for realism, yet they asked for pauses and expressive vocal quality, disliked push-to-talk, and said missing visual cues limited immersion; they also found text less nerve-wracking and better for reflection ([arXiv 2507.02122](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.02122v1)). A rapid review (three studies) found virtual patients not inferior to simulated patients for breaking bad news, with "lack of affect and interaction" as the barrier ([WUSTL summary](https://journalclub.wustl.edu/?p=323380)). I found no head-to-head trial of a human standardised patient against an AI patient in palliative care.

VR evidence on avatar body language is cautionary. A 229-student study (non-clinicians) varied avatar smile, nod and lean: smiling read as more attentive than leaning, avatars with no non-verbals produced more presence than ones with all three, and the authors said the findings should not yet inform training ([PMC12441729](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12441729)). So adding a body to the patient does not automatically help, and scripted gestures can backfire.

## Browser-only options give cheap cues and measurable silence, not presence

Most of what can ship in the existing Next.js and LiveKit stack works on words, timing and imagined action. Silence length after the patient stops speaking, talk-to-listen ratio, interruptions and speech rate come straight from the audio pipeline and need no camera. The PAL feedback suggests pauses and fillers in the patient's TTS would address a named complaint. Bracketed stage directions ("[looks at the window]") and trainee action buttons ("sit down", "take her hand", "stay quiet for a moment") that inject a text event to the LLM are cheap and logged for debrief. No study compares these with plain practice, and they risk letting trainees "claim" presence they did not enact, so the debrief should state the action was imagined (my inference, not sourced).

Webcam sensing is feasible but weakly validated. MediaPipe Face Landmarker runs client-side with landmarks, blendshapes and head-pose matrices ([Google AI Edge](https://developers.google.com/edge/mediapipe/solutions/vision/face_landmarker/web_js)), but it tracks iris landmarks without inferring gaze, and landmarks degrade beyond about 45 degrees of rotation ([TF blog](https://blog.tensorflow.org/2020/11/iris-landmark-tracking-in-browser-with-MediaPipe-and-TensorFlowJS.html?hl=pt_BR)). The best precedent, EQClinic (n=268, 2016), gave automated non-verbal feedback (nods, smiles, leaning, movement) and students who reviewed it scored modestly higher (13.21 vs 12.58, P=.04), but only 30% completed all components and no detection accuracy was reported ([PMC5037316](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5037316/)). Face-based emotion labelling is scientifically contested ([APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/ai-emotion-recognition-cant-be-trusted.html)), and Hume's Expression Measurement API closed on 14 June 2026 ([Hume docs](https://dev.hume.ai/docs/expression-measurement)), so do not build on it. A webcam also cannot measure distance or touch, and eye contact with a lens is not eye contact with a patient, so scoring it would mislead.

I found no product or paper in which an LLM patient demonstrably reacts to a trainee's posture. It is technically feasible: the Gemini Live API accepts webcam frames ([Google Cloud](https://docs.cloud.google.com/vertex-ai/generative-ai/docs/live-api/send-audio-video-streams)). The lower-risk design is on-device feature extraction that sends only a short text tag ("looking down for 8 s") to the patient LLM, which also limits the UK data-protection burden. Systems with biometric capability are likely high risk and need a DPIA ([NHS Wales IG guidance](https://dhcw.nhs.wales/ig/ig-documents/ig-toolkit-supporting-resources/requirement-guidance-documents/4-video-surveillance/), indirect, CCTV-oriented). I did not retrieve NHS England or ASPiH guidance.

## Avatars add a face at $0.11-0.37 a minute but not a body

LiveKit Agents lists 16 avatar plugins, including Anam, Tavus, HeyGen LiveAvatar, D-ID, Simli, bitHuman and Beyond Presence. The avatar joins the room as a participant, receives the agent's audio and publishes synced video, which fits the current stack ([LiveKit docs](https://docs.livekit.io/agents/models/avatar/)). Anam lists $0.11-0.16 per overage minute and zero data retention only on its enterprise tier ([Anam](https://anam.ai/pricing)). Tavus lists $0.32-0.37 per overage minute, SOC 2 and HIPAA on enterprise, and a perception model, Raven-1, whose capabilities I did not read ([Tavus](https://www.tavus.io/pricing)); a third-party aggregator quotes different numbers, so re-check before budgeting. A 20-minute session is therefore roughly **$2.40-7.40** on top of LLM, speech and LiveKit costs (my arithmetic). Latency claims (sub-second, 500-600 ms) are vendor claims ([Tavus blog](https://www.tavus.io/post/understanding-conversational-ai-latency-why-sub-second-matters)), whereas a 2024 prototype lagged up to 30 s ([arXiv 2405.19941](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.19941v1)).

The limit is control. These are audio-driven talking heads; I could not confirm that any accepts commands such as cry, look away or lie back. NVIDIA's open-sourced Audio2Face with MetaHuman gives richer expression but needs Unreal rendering or pixel streaming ([AWN](https://www.awn.com/news/nvidia-open-sources-audio2face-animation-model)). Evidence on realism is mixed: a Mayo Clinic pilot (n=30) saw no uncanny effect with a disclosed avatar, but sourcing was garbled, and a study of less-than-video-realistic avatars found lower trust ([arXiv 2411.09066](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.09066v3)). Virti already sells a screen-based AI patient for end-of-life conversations without a stated headset requirement, and Oxford Medical Simulation (a GBP 5M round in March 2026) markets emotional engines for breaking bad news, but neither publishes sensing of learner posture or distance ([Virti](https://virti.com/solutions/medical-training), [OMS blog](https://www.councilofdeans.org.uk/resource/sponsored-blog-ai-in-simulation-transforming-healthcare-training-innovation-month-2025-x-oms/)).

## Physical and VR routes deliver presence only when a real object or person does

Hybrid setups put the physical part in the room. PCS SimVox is a smart speaker behind a manikin's neck that gives it an AI voice, with vendor-claimed use at 100+ programmes but no outcome data ([HealthySimulation](https://www.healthysimulation.com/53646/pcs-simvox-smart-ai-speaker/)); Nasco ALEX Gen AI is a commercial torso simulator ([Pocket Nurse](https://www.pocketnurse.com/default/hf-11-81-7130p-nasco-alex-gen-ai-patient-communication-simulator-light-skin-tone)). I found no peer-reviewed study of an embedded AI voice for end-of-life bedside practice, so the benefit is by inference: the trainee must physically sit, which is the only behaviour with randomised patient-preference evidence. With LiveKit, a phone or tablet and a speaker at a skills-lab bed would do.

VR and haptics cost more and prove less. Headset programmes report cost as the main barrier (62.7% of educators in one snippet-sourced survey) and lack of VR training (54.7%) ([JMIR XR 2026](https://xr.jmir.org/2026/1/e52925/PDF)). Palliative VR exists: Brigham and Women's with Jolly Good ([Hospice News](https://hospicenews.com/2023/11/22/brigham-and-womens-hospital-develops-virtual-reality-hospice-palliative-training-partnership/)) and Wellstar's VR with live actors ([Wellstar](https://catalyst.wellstar.org/groundbreaking-compassionate-conversations-virtual-reality-training-debuts-at-hlth-2023/)), plus a code-status VR course claiming one hour instead of four (snippet only; [PMC10735823](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10735823/)). Platform privacy rules also limit sensing: Vision Pro does not share eye data or camera feeds with apps ([Apple](https://images.apple.com/legal/privacy/data/en/eyes-hands/)), and Quest 3 has no eye tracking ([PhoneArena](https://www.phonearena.com/news/why-does-quest-3-not-have-eye-tracking-explained_id155451)). Haptic social touch is a lab topic: a 12-person study found gloves and sleeves improved presence but vibration conveyed realistic sensation poorly ([arXiv 2502.13421](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.13421v1)). I found no outcome evidence that haptics improve communication training.

Safety applies to every route. Simulated-death guidance calls for psychological safety, a structured debrief and one-to-one follow-up for distress ([eCampusOntario](https://ecampusontario.pressbooks.pub/vlsvstoolkit/chapter/psychological-safety-2/)), and I found no AI-specific guidance or data on trainee distress with AI dying patients.

## Ranked suggestions

| Rank | Option | Tier | Evidence strength | Cost and hardware | Open questions |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Sit-in-a-real-room pilot: phone/tablet plus speaker at a skills-lab bed or two chairs, trainee instructed to sit, faculty observer or peer gives non-verbal feedback | Hybrid | Moderate for sitting itself (two RCT abstracts, patient perception); none for the setup | A speaker and an existing room; no headset | Does rehearsing posture with an AI voice transfer? Does a human observer add enough to justify staffing? |
| 2 | Pauses, fillers and stage directions in the patient's TTS and captions; silence-tolerance metric from audio | Browser quick win | Moderate for the need (PAL, n=17); none for the effect | Engineering time only; no extra hardware | Do stage directions improve realism or distract? What silence thresholds suit end-of-life talks? |
| 3 | Trainee action buttons ("sit down", "take her hand", "stay quiet") that inject events the patient reacts to, logged for debrief | Browser quick win | Weak, no comparative study | Engineering time; labelled as imagined in the debrief | Does it teach presence or give false credit? |
| 4 | Debrief hooks: reflective prompts about where the trainee physically was, a pause/opt-out control, signposting, no unannounced deaths | Browser quick win | Moderate (simulation-safety guidance); none AI-specific | Low | What do ASPiH and the trust require for AI dying-patient scenarios? |
| 5 | Opt-in on-device webcam prompts (face present, head down, lean proxy, stillness) as reflection cues, optionally a text tag to the patient LLM; no emotion labels, no scoring | Browser, medium | Weak (EQClinic 2016 only; no accuracy data) | MediaPipe is free; needs a DPIA | Webcam accuracy; trainee acceptance; whether feedback changes behaviour |
| 6 | Talking-head avatar through a LiveKit plugin (Anam first for cost and ZDR, Tavus for perception) | Avatar, medium | Weak to mixed (uncanny and expression evidence thin; no palliative study) | About $0.11-0.37 per minute; browser only | Can any provider do gaze, crying or head-down on command? UK residency? Does a face beat a good voice? |
| 7 | AI voice in a manikin (SimVox or ALEX) for fixed sim suites | Hybrid, higher | Weak, vendor claims only | Unpriced; hardware per suite | Prices and independent evaluation |
| 8 | MetaHuman plus Audio2Face or headset VR with an AI patient; haptics | High cost | Weak and cautionary (PMC12441729; haptics lab-only) | Quest-class headsets, GPU or streaming, hygiene logistics | Whether benefits justify cost; headset infection-control and procurement rules |

The recommended sequence is items 2, 3 and 4 as a first release, item 1 as the evaluation vehicle with the palliative team, and item 5 or 6 only if the pilot shows that learners want visual cues. Before committing money, verify: current Tavus, Anam and HeyGen pricing; whether any avatar API takes explicit expression commands; SimVox and ALEX prices; NHS England and ASPiH rules on AI simulation and trainee video; and the full text of the sitting RCTs, SOPHIE and the 2026 breaking-bad-news virtual-patient paper (arXiv 2607.22118), which I could not read.

## Conclusion

The sharpest finding is that presence cannot be shipped through the screen; it has to be enacted by the trainee in a real space, and the software's job is to cue it, make silence and timing tangible, and capture reflection. The team's feedback therefore points to a combined product rather than a richer avatar, and the evidence says an avatar alone is the least proven way to meet it.

The larger gap is research, not engineering. Nobody has shown that any of these options changes how trainees behave with real dying patients, so the pilot should measure observer-rated non-verbal behaviour, sitting and silence tolerance, and trainee distress against a human-actor control. That would both settle the team's concern and fill a gap the literature leaves open.
